Advisory · detailed 21 October 2026

The eMudhra certificate authority compromise

Private keys for eight eMudhra intermediate CA certificates — six for TLS, two for code signing — were found to be recoverable due to weak key generation. eMudhra revoked the certificates and stopped issuing under the affected G3 root.

The facts

What is known, as published

What happenedA certificate authority key compromise — not a software vulnerability in eMudhra products. Weak key generation made the private keys recoverable.
Affected materialEight intermediate CA certificates: six TLS, two code signing. Certificates were revoked; issuance under the affected G3 root stopped.
CVE-2026-111943The Go ecosystem mitigation: crypto/x509 adds a denylist so Certificate.Verify rejects chains involving the affected intermediates. Go lists affected versions as before 1.26.10 and 1.27.0 prereleases before 1.27.3.
Exploitation statusCisco Talos reported no malicious incidents involving the certificates in the wild as of 21 October, 14:00 UTC.
Conditions for harmA vulnerable verifier must trust an affected certificate, and an attacker must be in a position to present a forged certificate while intercepting or redirecting the connection. Red Hat states the key compromise does not by itself mean systems were compromised.
Do not conflate this with PHP2Shell. Different mechanism, different remediation, different exploitation picture. In your briefing, keep them as two rows — one patching item, one certificate-hygiene item — and resist any narrative that merges them into a single apocalyptic event.
Your exposure

Auditing your chains is mostly plumbing

List your issuers

Every public host, every certificate, who issued it and when. Most teams discover a wildcard from 2021 they had forgotten about.

Check against revocations

Cross-reference issuers and intermediates against the revoked set, and confirm your trust stores and Go binaries are on the fixed versions.

Re-issue where needed

Replace certificates chaining through affected intermediates, then re-scan to prove every host now presents a clean chain.

What Sentinel checks for you: TLS and certificate chains are analyzed on every live host in your assessment — validity, issuer, and chain integrity — so an affected intermediate shows up as a named finding against the host that serves it, not as a vague "consider your certificates."
